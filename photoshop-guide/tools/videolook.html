10.0

about

The companion to FilmLook for television. It does not paint a look on: it turns the photo into a real video signal at the standard's own resolution (575 lines of about 922 samples for PAL, sampled at four times the colour subcarrier), passes it through a camera, a transmission channel, a tape machine and any transfer, then decodes it with the chosen receiver, so cross-colour, dot crawl, NTSC hue errors, PAL desaturation, Hanover bars and SECAM streaks come out of the maths as they did in reality. A display stage then rebuilds the picture at document size with scan lines, shadow mask or aperture grille, glow and tube geometry, or as a clean frame-grab, or as a photograph of the screen. The numbers come from the standards themselves (ITU-R BT.470, the UK System I specification, FCC and SMPTE documents) and from BBC engineering sources: the 1964 BBC camera comparisons, the 1967 registration report, BBC training sheets on camera processing, tape and colour-under recording, receivers and the 1967 standards converter. Settings whose defaults are estimates are marked est. on the panel. Version 1.1 adds the receiving set as a stage of its own: a set built for another country's system (French System L comes out as a rolling negative on a UK set), co-channel interference from a second picture, overload, and the set's sync, so pictures tear and roll for real reasons. It also adds Soviet, East German, Japanese, Brazilian and Argentine systems, Hi-Vision, the pre-war systems (Baird 30 and 240 lines, Berlin and NBC 441, Moscow 343), CBS colour-wheel TV, Apollo 11, slow-scan TV, Pixelvision, CCTV, LaserDisc, CED, Canal+ scrambling and teletext. Version 1.2 adds the off-screen photograph as a proper stage: a shutter that catches one field or a whole frame, a black-and-white negative and print, flare, soft dust and the tube surround, together with two faults of older sets that turn dark captions grey (mean-level AGC and no DC restorer). The result goes on a new layer; the photo is untouched. Version 1.3 turns to the sharper, harsher end. A new Picture source stage draws the photo as a terminal, a home computer or a teletext decoder could have drawn it: character pictures on the DEC VT05 and VT100 and the IBM monochrome display, CGA and BBC Micro palettes, teletext mosaics and a Tektronix storage tube, each on its own raster, with monochrome phosphors (P1, P3, P4, P31, P39) whose colours are worked out from their published spectra. High-resolution CCTV gets 570-line cameras on 1000-line monitors, wide-angle lenses and an infrared night mode; Rediffusion's wired television, studio RGB monitors and sets with scan velocity modulation cover the sharp end of home viewing. A Digital stream stage breaks pictures the way MPEG-2 and H.264 decoders do, on real 16 x 16 macroblocks and 8 x 8 DCT blocks: lost keyframes and datamoshing, slice errors that tile, tint and jumble the rows, and H.264 error smear. Version 1.4 widens the field again. Pay-TV scrambling adds Sky's VideoCrypt cut-and-rotate (and what a home-built decoder made of it), the BBC Select line shuffles and Nagravision Syster. The Picture source stage gains the video synthesisers of the early 1970s: a Rutt/Etra-style scan processor that lifts each line by its brightness, Paik/Abe scan modulation and the magnet on the tube, Scanimate and Sandin-style colourisers, and video feedback. A flat-panel display replaces the tube with a Game Boy LCD, pocket LCD televisions, laptop STN screens, orange gas plasma, LED signs or a flip-disc board, and new cameras cover night vision from the wartime infrared converter to Gen 3, and thermal imagers with White Hot, Black Hot, Ironbow, Rainbow, Arctic and Lava palettes. Version 1.5 adds three more areas. The Digital stream stage gains a starved videophone and web codec in the manner of H.261 (1990): QCIF and CIF videophones, the grey QuickCam and CU-SeeMe, RealVideo over a 28.8k modem and 3GP phone clips, with only the most-changed blocks sent so old and new picture break up together. The Picture source stage gains a vector display that traces the picture's contours as beam strokes, in white, colour, oscilloscope green or on a radar tube. A new Screen to paper stage puts the finished picture through a machine that printed it: a dye-sublimation colour video printer, a black-and-white medical video printer, a Group 3 fax with dither and line errors, a 1930s wirephoto, or a NOAA weather satellite's APT line with its sync bars and telemetry wedges. Version 1.6 reaches beyond television to other imaging instruments, all under Picture source: space-probe cameras from TIROS-1 to Voyager (few pixels, few bits, reseau dots, lost telemetry, colour from filtered frames, Mariner 4's picture coloured in crayon on printed numbers) and Lunar Orbiter's film read out in strips; the Landsat multispectral scanner in false colour or single bands, striped in sixes; radar screens with a turning sweep on a P7 tube, weather radar, side-scan sonar and echo sounders; ultrasound, CT (really projected and reconstructed), MRI with its ringing, wrap-around and ghosts, the X-ray image intensifier and the gamma camera; and the scanning electron and atomic force microscopes. In each one the photo stands in for what the instrument saw. Version 1.7 adds the first digital cameras and camera phones (Dycam, QuickTake, QV-10, the Game Boy Camera, Mavica, early camera phones: few pixels, a colour mosaic, JPEG blocks), hand-held and flatbed scanners and slit-scan photo-finish cameras under Picture source; and under Screen to paper, instant film (SX-70, 600, Spectra, peel-apart, Instax, black and white, with manipulation, expiry and roller marks), the line printer and teleprinter (pictures made of overprinted characters), the 9-pin dot-matrix printer, the pen plotter, the thermal receipt printer, and pictures by wire and radio from before television: Bartlane cable pictures, the Fultograph and the Hellschreiber. Version 1.8 adds pictures that came down phone lines and radio: Prestel and Minitel mosaic pages, Telidon and NAPLPS pictures drawn as flat shapes, amateur slow-scan TV (Macdonald's 120 lines, Robot, Martin and Scottie modes, with static, fading, slant and pictures cut short), HF radiofax photos and charts, and the early web: GIFs in the browser-safe 216 colours or their own palettes, interlaced GIFs and progressive JPEGs caught part-way, half-loaded JPEGs, and JPEGs dithered on a 256-colour screen.

quick start

  1. 01Open Plugins › VideoLook and choose a look. The note under the list says what the chain is and what it rests on.
  2. 02Draw a marquee and press Preview selection to see just that area. The whole frame still goes through the signal chain, so the preview is exact.
  3. 03Press Render. The look goes on a new VideoLook · layer at the top; Strength is that layer's opacity.
  4. 04Adjust any stage below and press Re-render last. Tick boxes on the section headers switch whole stages on and off. Save combinations under My presets.

10.3

workflow

film on television

For 1970s drama exteriors or any film shown on TV, render the FilmLook stock first, then apply VideoLook's telecine look to the result.

long-distance reception

Summer lift, French TV on a UK set, Sporadic-E: pick the look, then move Co-channel strength, Roll position and Horizontal hold, and press New noise for another moment. For a real second station, add a layer named second picture.

off-screen photographs

For a 1960s station symbol or caption as it survives in off-screen photographs: start with white artwork on black, choose a look from Photographed off the screen, then move Print exposure (grey and ghostly, or burnt out), Softness under Camera, and Mean-level AGC and Black follows the picture under Decoder & set. See the Look Cookbook.

terminals and computers

Character pictures read best from pictures with strong shapes and plain backgrounds. Raise Contrast before conversion under Picture source if a face goes to blank cells; try Screen close-up and then Photo of the screen for a period print.

broken streams

For a datamosh between two pictures, put the earlier scene on a layer named second picture and choose Datamosh from a second picture. Change Frames since the error and the Movement sliders, and press New noise for another break-up.

video synthesisers and panels

Scan-processor looks work best on pictures with large light and dark shapes. Chain a second instrument by trying the colouriser after a scan-processor look. Panel looks crop the picture to the panel's own shape, so move the subject with Shift image if it falls outside.

videophones, vectors and paper

Videophone looks are tiny pictures enlarged, so they read best at a distance. Vector looks need clear light and dark shapes; raise the detail for busy pictures. Screen to paper works on the whole picture, so a preview of a selection still renders everything first and takes a little longer.

instruments

The instruments read the photo as their subject: brightness becomes echo strength, height, density or activity, and edges become echoes. Strong shapes and clear contrast work best. Set Frame to Fill the document to use the whole canvas; the round screens (radar, CT, image intensifier) then sit in the middle. Put a radar look on the P7 tube, or send an ultrasound to the B&W video printer under Screen to paper, to finish it the way it was seen.

print-outs and early digital

The print-outs run on the finished picture at the document's size, so a large document gives the line printer, plotter and Hell tapes room to show their marks; a small one makes them coarse. Instant film fills the document unless you turn its frame on. For a Game Boy Camera picture on its own printer, combine the camera with the receipt printer, as the look of that name does.

videotex, radio and web

These looks redraw the photo the way the system carried it, at the system's own small size, so they look the same whatever the document size. Prestel and Minitel look best on their screens (the looks that end in a set or tube); the clean versions suit graphic work. The loading looks (part-drawn pages, first GIF pass, first JPEG scan) are snapshots of a picture still arriving.

evidence

Ten looks (Alexandra Palace 1937, 405-line at home, US live 1950, French 819-line, Moscow 343, Baird 240, Berlin and NBC 441, CBS colour, Belgian 819) are built from the specifications alone, because no authentic pictures survive. The rest are research-based and can be calibrated against reference frames.

10.4

gotchas

  • Large documents take a while: a 24-megapixel render with a CRT view is roughly 10–20 seconds. Preview selection is quicker.
  • The Tektronix storage-tube look works on a 2048-sample raster and takes several times longer than the other terminal looks.
  • A Photo of the screen is made from the whole picture (flare, grain, dust, tilt), so its previews take as long as a full render, and a one-field photo draws the screen twice.
  • The photo is treated as sRGB; convert documents in other profiles first.
  • A still cannot show movement: the 60-field 'video look' of US sitcoms, dot crawl moving and interlace flicker are not there. Implied motion shows what a single frame of movement would look like.
  • 32-bit documents are not supported.
  • Hi-Vision has no camera stage (HD cameras aren't modelled) and needs Implied motion with 'Only the selection moves' to show its motion blur.
